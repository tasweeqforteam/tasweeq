The Quiet City

Most streets are designed around a single number: the speed of the car that will use them. Lane widths, corner radii and sight lines all follow from it. Change that number and almost everything else about the street can change with it, including how long people are willing to stay.

A street built for thirty kilometres an hour needs less asphalt than one built for fifty. The surplus width is the opportunity. It can hold trees, which bring shade and make a summer walk possible. It can hold benches, which give older residents a reason to stop. It can hold a wider pavement, which lets two people walk side by side and talk.

Narrow first, then furnish

The order of work matters. Painting new markings on a wide carriageway rarely slows anyone down; drivers read the space, not the paint. Physically narrowing the road with kerbs, planting or parking bays changes behaviour because it changes what feels comfortable.

Once the street is narrower, furniture can follow: seating facing the sun in winter and away from it in summer, drinking fountains at corners, and lighting at the height of a face rather than a lorry.

Shade is infrastructure

In hot climates, shade decides whether a street is walkable at all. A continuous canopy, from trees or from buildings with deep arcades, turns a route that people avoid at noon into one they use all day. Shade should be planned as carefully as drainage, and measured as an essential part of the street rather than added as decoration.

None of this requires a new city. It asks existing streets to be redesigned one block at a time, starting where people already want to walk.

Speed is a design decision. Every metre of asphalt is an invitation to drive faster.
